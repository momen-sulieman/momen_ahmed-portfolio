A delivery app usually treats a bakery as a menu with a pin on a map. But taking
orders is the easy half. The harder half is that a small bakery often can't say
precisely what it produced last week, how much of it went in the bin, or what it
owes the person who started at four in the morning.

Bakemart does both halves in one app: a storefront customers order from, and the
bookkeeping the owner actually runs the business on.

## One app, three roles

The bottom bar has three tabs — **Work**, **Home**, **My bakery** — and that's the
whole product in one line. The same account can order bread, deliver someone
else's order, and own the bakery that baked it. There's no separate driver app
and no separate merchant app.

I went that way because the alternative asks a lot of a small market: three
listings, three installs, three accounts, and a customer who has to know which
one to download. One app means one install and one identity.

The cost is real, though. Every screen has to answer "who is this person right
now," the build carries code most users never open, and the permission surface is
wider than it would be if each role shipped separately. It's a trade I'd make
again at this size, and probably reverse if the delivery side ever got big enough
to deserve its own release cycle.

## Delivery without the employment

Anyone can switch on **Receive orders**, set how far they're willing to travel,
and start accepting jobs. There's no application, no shift, and no contract —
turn the toggle off and you're done for the day.

The app takes no commission on the order. It calculates the delivery fee and
nothing else, so the bakery keeps its margin and the driver keeps the fee. That
rules out the obvious revenue model, which is a deliberate choice rather than an
oversight: the thing that makes it worth installing in a small market is that it
doesn't tax either side of a transaction they were already making.

The constraints that remain are practical ones — a radius in kilometres, a cap on
how many orders one person can hold at once, and a QR scan at pickup so the
handoff between bakery and driver is confirmed by both ends rather than assumed.

## The half nobody sees

The owner side is where most of the thinking went. Owners link workers to the
bakery and record production, sales and attendance against them, which rolls up
into revenue, expenses, waste and net profit.

The part I find most useful is also the least obvious: **a linked worker doesn't
have to be a Bakemart user.** At least one real account anchors the bakery, but
the rest can be virtual profiles that exist purely so payroll and attendance have
somewhere to live. A bakery with six staff and one smartphone between them can
still keep accurate books.

The trade-off is that a virtual worker's record is the owner's word — there's no
second party confirming attendance the way a real account could. I took that
deal because the alternative is worse: requiring everyone to have an account
means the books simply don't get kept.

Inventory follows from the same records. Production quantities update as cycles
are entered, so stock is a consequence of what was logged rather than a separate
number someone has to remember to correct.

## Why data entry got the most attention

Bookkeeping features fail for a boring reason: if logging a bake takes a minute,
nobody logs the bake, and by the end of the week the numbers are fiction.

So entry speed was treated as the actual feature. Every number an owner types has
to be fast enough to do mid-shift with flour on their hands, because the accuracy
of everything downstream — waste, profit, payroll, stock — depends entirely on
whether the entry happened at all.

## Where it is now

It's an ongoing build on Flutter and Dart with Supabase behind it, and it ships
Arabic and English with a one-tap switch, since the users I'm building for don't
all read the same interface.

Still open: the owner-side reporting deserves more than running totals, and the
pickup handoff is the piece I'd most want real drivers to stress-test before
calling it finished.
